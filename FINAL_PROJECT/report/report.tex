\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Project Scope}

For a prospective student, a university's website is often the only source of information
available before applying. It decides whether they can find the programmes on offer, learn the
admission requirements, discover a deadline, or contact someone. A badly organised site is not
merely an aesthetic failing; it is a barrier to access.

Most existing tools for assessing university website quality are rule-based: an analyst fixes a
set of weights and the tool applies arithmetic. This project builds a machine-learning framework
instead. It extracts landing-page attributes automatically, converts them into a documented
quality score, and trains a range of regression algorithms to predict that score from the raw
attributes. We apply it to \nUni{} university websites worldwide, then test it on one country,
Bangladesh, whose universities are kept out of training by construction.

\subsection{Problem Statement}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{c@{\hspace{7pt}}c@{\hspace{7pt}}c@{\hspace{7pt}}c@{\hspace{7pt}}c}
\fbox{\parbox{3.1cm}{\centering\small\textbf{INPUT}\\[2pt]\nFeat{} measured\\attributes of one\\university landing page}}
& $\longrightarrow$ &
\fbox{\parbox{2.9cm}{\centering\small\textbf{MODEL}\\[2pt]trained\\regressor}}
& $\longrightarrow$ &
\fbox{\parbox{3.7cm}{\centering\small\textbf{OUTPUT}\\[2pt]website score $0$--$100$\\grade A+ to F\\global / regional / country rank}}
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent The task is supervised regression: given the attribute vector of a landing page, predict
its website quality score. Since the deliverable is a league table, we treat rank agreement
(Spearman $\rho$) as a primary metric alongside $R^2$ and mean absolute error.

\subsection{Objectives}

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.3cm,style=nextline,itemsep=2pt]
\item[O1.] Collect and clean a global dataset of university landing-page attributes, and classify
every attribute by page region, measurement type and scoring role.
\item[O2.] Construct a transparent, reproducible label that turns those attributes into a 0--100
quality score, stated as equations, using every attribute for which a reason to use it can be
given.
\item[O3.] Split the data 80/20, holding an entire country out of training, and train a broad range
of regression algorithms on identical inputs.
\item[O4.] Compare their performance and explain why they differ.
\item[O5.] Evaluate the framework on \nBD{} Bangladeshi universities the model has never seen, and
characterise where those websites gain and lose points.
\item[O6.] Produce global, regional and country rankings, and measure how much of the ranking is a
property of the websites rather than of our own weighting judgement.
\end{description}

\noindent\textbf{Deliverables:} the cleaned and scored dataset, the attribute catalogue, an executed
analysis notebook, model artefacts with a scoring entry point, the ranking tables, and this report.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
\item A seven-dimension scoring model with a documented derivation, explicit equations, published
inner weights, two gating rules and six non-linear response curves (Section~\ref{sec:label}).
\item A classification of all \nFeat{} attributes on three axes, specified in
Table~\ref{tab:catalog}, with a stated reason for each of the \nUnscored{} attributes the label does
not use (Section~\ref{sec:exclusions}).
\item A controlled comparison of twelve regression algorithms across six model families
(Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\item A country-holdout case study in which all \nBD{} Bangladeshi universities are excluded from
training and scored as unseen data (Section~\ref{sec:bangladesh}).
\item An error analysis tracing the performance gap between model families to a specific mechanism
rather than to capacity in the abstract (Section~\ref{sec:why}).
\item A sensitivity and ablation study measuring how much of the ranking survives a different
analyst's weights, and which blocks of attributes the model needs
(Sections~\ref{sec:ablation}--\ref{sec:sensitivity}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:lit}

Work on university website quality has used automated web analysis, questionnaire studies,
usability assessment and web-quality models. Table~\ref{tab:lit} summarises the studies closest to
ours, with attention to those evaluating sites through automatically measurable content, usability,
accessibility, performance and technical attributes.

\input{tables/tab_litreview}

\noindent Two points from this literature shaped our design. Saleh \emph{et al.}~\cite{saleh2022}
report that no universally accepted model of university website quality exists, so there is no
external label to borrow and one has to be constructed and stated in full
(Section~\ref{sec:label}). Rashida \emph{et al.}~\cite{rashida2021} and Mukanda \emph{et
al.}~\cite{mukanda2022} both aggregate measured attributes using weights the analyst fixes, and
neither reports what the ranking would look like under different weights. We measure that directly
in Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}.

\section{Data Collection}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Population and Frame}

The dataset was built from university websites in six geographical regions. We crawled 1,230
landing pages. The collection instrument specified 75 candidate attributes; six of them
(\texttt{a19}, \texttt{a52}, \texttt{a55}, \texttt{a56}, \texttt{a64}, \texttt{a68}) could not be
extracted reliably and are absent from the data. We did not impute them, leaving \textbf{69
attributes per university}.

Collection was distributed across six collectors, one per region. One consequence recurs throughout
this report: region and collector are perfectly confounded, so any regional difference in a measured
quantity is partly a property of the websites and partly a property of who measured them.

\subsection{Landing-Page Attributes Used for Evaluation}

The attributes are limited to information visible on, or directly verifiable from, the landing page.
Most are Boolean values recording the presence or absence of a page element, which is
scraper-friendly and allows reliable DOM- and keyword-based detection. Integer attributes record
counts such as navigation items, events, accreditation badges and broken links. Float attributes
record continuous measurements such as page-load speed, colour contrast ratio, mobile responsiveness
and alt-text coverage.

Table~\ref{tab:catalog} is the full specification. Attributes are grouped by the block of
Section~\ref{sec:blocks}. \emph{Dim} names the dimension of Section~\ref{sec:label} that consumes
the attribute, or a dash where it does not enter the label, in which case the reason appears in the
justification column; \emph{Pts} is $w_k \lambda_{k,j}$, the points the attribute contributes at its
maximum. The provenance keys record where each attribute comes from, with those marked \emph{New}
added from direct observation of live landing pages rather than taken from prior work.

\subsection{Attribute Selection and Collection Protocol}

Ranking attributes such as QS World Ranking and Webometrics were recorded as nullable, since not
every university displays them, and the most recent notice date was stored in ISO 8601 format where
available. Two categories, \emph{Rankings \& Recognition} and \emph{Events \& Media}, were added to
the schema after inspecting real landing pages rather than taken from prior work.

For each university we used the verified official domain as the starting URL. Boolean attributes
were encoded as
\begin{equation}
x_i = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if the attribute is detected,}\\
                    0, & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}
\end{equation}
counts as integers, continuous measurements as floating-point values, and unavailable nullable
measurements as null. The scraper read HTML and DOM elements, hyperlinks, metadata, text content,
images, media elements, accessibility properties and technical response information. Pages needing
JavaScript were rendered in a browser environment before extraction. Requests respected
\texttt{robots.txt}; the crawler submitted no forms and took no action that modifies a target site.

\noindent Dropping the external ranking values is the one place where we discarded a predictor that
would have helped. QS and Webometrics ranks correlate with our score, but they measure institutional

must have a good website, which is the inference this project exists to avoid.
Section~\ref{sec:prestige} shows what that inference would have cost.

\subsection{Missing and Nullable Values}

Three attributes are missing for some universities, and the usual remedy of replacing them with the
column median is wrong for all three. The median of \texttt{notice\_recency\_days} is one day, so
median-filling would record the 468 universities with no dated notice anywhere on their site as
having posted something yesterday, handing the strongest freshness signal in the dataset to the
institutions that earned it least. Because absence has a known direction in each case, we fill at
the worst defensible value and compute a \texttt{*\_was\_missing} indicator \emph{before} the fill,
so that ``never measured'' stays recoverable.

\subsection{Final Dataset Structure and Feature Blocks}
\label{sec:blocks}

After cleaning and feature engineering each university is one feature vector, and the modelling
dataset is
\begin{equation}
X \in \mathbb{R}^{\,\nUni{} \times \nFeat{}}, \qquad y \in [0,100]^{\,\nUni{}},

an attribute could be measured at all, which is a property of our extraction rather than of the

\subsection{Modelling Representation}

Three groups of columns are withheld from the model. Identification and crawl metadata, namely
\texttt{uni\_id}, \texttt{name}, \texttt{url}, \texttt{final\_url}, \texttt{http\_status},
\texttt{fetched\_at} and \texttt{member}, are not properties of the website. Raw
\texttt{load\_time\_s} is replaced by its within-region standardisation. Most importantly, the
intermediate outputs of the scoring curves (\texttt{nav\_quality}, \texttt{notice\_evidence},
\texttt{event\_evidence}, \texttt{load\_time\_pct\_region}) are withheld, because supplying them
would hand the model the answer. It receives the raw observables instead, such as
\texttt{a03\_nav\_item\_count} and \texttt{a24\_event\_count}, and has to recover the threshold,
saturation and gate behaviour on its own.

All \nFeat{} remaining features go to every model, including the \nUnscored{} that do not enter the
label, so the model must separate an attribute that matters from one that merely accompanies
mattering. Section~\ref{sec:ablation} shows that it does.

\noindent Three properties of the data shape the modelling that follows. The dimensions are only

bimodal rather than bell-shaped: the shoulder near 45 in Figure~\ref{fig:target} is the population
of sites hitting a scoring gate, and it is what separates the algorithm families in

\section{Label Construction}
\label{sec:label}

\subsection{The Need for a Constructed Label}

The dataset records what a website has, not how good it is. There is no column called ``quality'',
and as the review literature reports, no accepted model to borrow one from. Published university
rankings measure research output and reputation rather than websites, so using them would mean
training a model to predict prestige from page structure.

A label therefore has to be constructed. It is the most consequential design decision here and the
one most open to disagreement, so this section states how the dimensions were derived, how the
weights were set, what the equations are, which attributes are deliberately left out, and what the
known weaknesses of the result are.

\subsection{Derivation of the Seven Dimensions}
\label{sec:derivation}

We did not obtain the dimensions by clustering the attributes or by factor analysis. They come from
a task decomposition driven by one question: what does a prospective student need from a university
website? The applicant's path through a landing page breaks into seven sub-tasks; each becomes a
dimension, and the attributes serving it become its terms.

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\caption{Derivation of the seven dimensions from the applicant's task.}
\begin{tabular}{cp{4.15cm}p{5.15cm}cc}
\toprule
& \textbf{The applicant's sub-task} & \textbf{Attributes that serve it} & \textbf{Blocks} & \textbf{Dim.} \\
\midrule
1 & ``What can I study here, and who will teach me?'' & programmes listing, department links,
faculty page, research highlight, library, careers & B5 & $D_1$ \\[2pt]
2 & ``How do I apply, what does it cost, and who do I ask?'' & admissions policy, admission notices,
scholarships, prospectus, contact page, FAQ, live chat & B3, B5, B8 & $D_2$ \\[2pt]
3 & ``Is this institution actually alive and running?'' & notice recency, notice board and timestamp,
dated events, news, calendar, last-updated line & B3, B4, B6 & $D_3$ \\[2pt]
4 & ``Can I find any of the above?'' & primary navigation, menu breadth, search box, breadcrumb,
quick links, footer sitemap & B1, B6 & $D_4$ \\[2pt]
5 & ``Can I read and use this, on the phone I own?'' & mobile score, contrast ratio, alt-text
coverage, accessible design, language toggle, accessibility toggle & B1, B7, B9, B11 & $D_5$ \\[2pt]
6 & ``Does the site work, can it be found, and is it safe?'' & HTTPS, broken links, load speed,
compression, page title and meta, sitemap, favicon & B9, B10 & $D_6$ \\[2pt]
7 & ``Is it clear who these people are?'' & vision/mission, about text, footer contact,
accreditation, student portal, alumni, achievements, logo, social links, trust seal &
B1, B2, B5, B6, B8 & $D_7$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent Three rules governed the assignment, and they are what make the mapping reproducible
rather than a matter of taste. First, one attribute belongs to one dimension, assigned to the
sub-task it primarily serves: \texttt{a48\_footer\_sitemap} sits in the footer block but serves
finding things, so $D_4$ scores it and $D_7$ does not, since double-counting would quietly re-weight
the label. Second, an attribute serving no sub-task is not scored, but the reason must be stated;
Section~\ref{sec:exclusions} gives it for each of the \nUnscored{} excluded attributes. Third, only
attributes that are actually populated are used: anything missing for more than half the dataset was
dropped in cleaning, and anything present on fewer than 2\% of sites is excluded as too rare to
carry weight.

\subsection{Weight Assignment}

The seven dimensions combine through weights summing to 100, set by a stated ordering principle
rather than by tuning: a dimension's weight is proportional to how directly it serves the applicant's
purpose in visiting the site. That yields three tiers.

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\caption{The three weighting tiers.}
\begin{tabular}{lp{7.9cm}cc}
\toprule
\textbf{Tier} & \textbf{Principle} & \textbf{Dimensions} & \textbf{Points} \\
\midrule
\textbf{The content}    & What the applicant came for. If it is absent, nothing else rescues the
visit. & $D_1, D_2$ & \textbf{50} \\
\textbf{The delivery}   & The machinery that makes the content reachable, current and usable.
Necessary, but in service of the content. & $D_3, D_4, D_5$ & \textbf{40} \\
\textbf{The foundation} & Baseline hygiene, discoverability and self-presentation. Expected rather
than rewarded; their absence is punished by the gates instead. & $D_6, D_7$ & \textbf{10} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\noindent Within a tier the same principle sets the order. $D_1$ outranks $D_2$ because a student
who cannot find the programme has no reason to read the admission rules, and $D_4$ outranks $D_3$
and $D_5$ because navigation conditions access to everything else. These are not the only defensible
weights, and we do not claim they are; we claim that a stated rule fixed them before any score was
computed, that they are published in full, and that the league table barely moves when they are
perturbed (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}).

\subsection{The Scoring Equation}
\label{sec:equations}

Each dimension is a sub-score $D_k \in [0,1]$, and the website score is their weighted sum subject to
a cap:

\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;
\text{Score} \;=\; \min\!\left( \sum_{k=1}^{7} w_k D_k,\;\; \text{cap} \right),
\qquad \sum_{k=1}^{7} w_k = 100 \;}
\label{eq:score}
\end{equation}

\noindent Each dimension is itself a weighted sum of transformed attributes,

\begin{equation}
D_k \;=\; \sum_{j} \lambda_{k,j}\, \phi_{k,j}\!\left(a_{k,j}\right),
\qquad \sum_j \lambda_{k,j} = 1,
\label{eq:dim}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $a_{k,j}$ is a measured attribute, $\phi_{k,j}$ the transform applied to it (the
identity for a binary flag, otherwise one of the response curves of Section~\ref{sec:curves}), and
$\lambda_{k,j}$ its weight inside the dimension. Since the inner weights sum to one, dimension $k$
contributes at most $w_k$ points and attribute $j$ of dimension $k$ is worth exactly
$w_k \lambda_{k,j}$ points at its maximum. Writing $g(a)$ for an attribute read as $0/1$:

\begin{align}
D_1 &= 0.30\,g(\text{programs}) + 0.25\,g(\text{departments}) + 0.15\,g(\text{faculty}) \notag\\
    &\quad + 0.12\,g(\text{library}) + 0.10\,g(\text{research}) + 0.08\,g(\text{careers})
    \label{eq:d1}\\[3pt]
D_2 &= 0.26\,g(\text{admissions policy}) + 0.20\,g(\text{admission notice})
       + 0.18\,g(\text{scholarship}) \notag\\
    &\quad + 0.15\,g(\text{contact}) + 0.12\,g(\text{prospectus})
       + 0.06\,g(\text{FAQ}) + 0.03\,g(\text{live chat})
    \label{eq:d2}\\[3pt]
D_3 &= 0.32\,f_{\text{rec}}(\text{notice age})
       + 0.20\,f_{\text{evt}}(\text{event count},\text{dated})
       + 0.16\,g(\text{news}) \notag\\
    &\quad + 0.12\,g(\text{calendar}) + 0.08\,g(\text{notice board})
       + 0.06\,g(\text{timestamp}) \notag\\
    &\quad + 0.04\,g(\text{upcoming events}) + 0.02\,g(\text{last-updated line})
    \label{eq:d3}\\[3pt]
D_4 &= 0.25\,g(\text{primary nav}) + 0.25\,f_{\text{nav}}(\text{menu items})
       + 0.20\,g(\text{search}) \notag\\
    &\quad + 0.10\,g(\text{breadcrumb}) + 0.10\,g(\text{quick links})
       + 0.10\,g(\text{footer sitemap})
    \label{eq:d4}\\[3pt]
D_5 &= 0.32\,\frac{\text{mobile score}}{100} + 0.23\,f_{\text{con}}(\text{contrast})
       + 0.18\,f_{\text{alt}}(\text{alt-text \%}) \notag\\
    &\quad + 0.10\,g(\text{accessible design}) + 0.09\,g(\text{language toggle})
       + 0.08\,g(\text{a11y toggle})
    \label{eq:d5}\\[3pt]
D_6 &= 0.26\,g(\text{https}) + 0.24\,f_{\text{brk}}(\text{broken links})
       + 0.20\,\bigl(1 - \mathrm{pctl}_{\text{region}}(\text{load time})\bigr) \notag\\

\subsection{Non-Linear Response Curves}
\label{sec:curves}

Six attributes do not reward linearly, and the label says so explicitly rather than assuming
proportionality. Each transform is chosen because the attribute's relationship with quality is
genuinely not monotone or not proportional.

\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\textwidth]{figures/fig_curves.png}
\caption{Four of the response curves. The remaining two are $f_{\text{evt}}$
(Equation~\eqref{eq:evt}) and the concave $f_{\text{alt}}$ (Equation~\eqref{eq:alt}).}
\end{figure}

\vspace{-4pt}
\paragraph{Menu breadth, an inverted U.} A menu of 0--2 items leads nowhere, 5--9 items indicates an
organised site, and 20 items is a wall of links. Neither ``more is better'' nor ``less is better''
describes this, so no monotone function can represent it. The curve enters $D_4$:
\begin{equation}
f_{\text{nav}}(n) = \begin{cases}
0.00 & n \le 0\\ 0.30 & 1 \le n \le 2\\ 0.75 & 3 \le n \le 4\\
\mathbf{1.00} & \mathbf{5 \le n \le 9}\\ 0.90 & 10 \le n \le 12\\
0.75 & 13 \le n \le 15\\ 0.55 & n \ge 16
\end{cases}
\label{eq:nav}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Notice age, a staleness decay.} A post from last week and one from last year are not 51
weeks apart in value; ``recent'' and ``abandoned'' are plateaus with a cliff between them. This term
carries the largest single weight in $D_3$:
\begin{equation}
f_{\text{rec}}(d) = \begin{cases}
1.00 & d \le 7 \text{ days}\\ 0.90 & d \le 30\\ 0.70 & d \le 90\\
0.45 & d \le 180\\ 0.20 & d \le 365\\ 0.00 & d > 365
\end{cases}
\label{eq:rec}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Contrast, a plateau at WCAG AAA.} The accessibility standard is 7:1, and beyond it more
contrast does not improve reading, since 21:1 is no better than 12:1. The curve therefore stops
rewarding instead of rising:
\begin{equation}
f_{\text{con}}(r) = \begin{cases}
0.30\,u(r;1,3) & r < 3.0\\
0.30 + 0.30\,u(r;3,4.5) & 3.0 \le r < 4.5\\
0.60 + 0.40\,u(r;4.5,7) & 4.5 \le r < 7.0\\
1.00 & r \ge 7.0
\end{cases}
\qquad u(x;l,h) = \frac{x-l}{h-l}\bigg|_0^1
\label{eq:con}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Broken links, a step penalty.} One dead link is an oversight and forty is neglect, so the
penalty is not proportional to the count:
\begin{equation}
f_{\text{brk}}(n) = \begin{cases}
1.00 & n = 0\\ 0.85 & 1 \le n \le 2\\ 0.65 & 3 \le n \le 5\\
0.40 & 6 \le n \le 15\\ 0.15 & 16 \le n \le 40\\ 0.00 & n > 40
\end{cases}
\label{eq:brk}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Events, a count scaled by whether they are dated.} Twenty undated ``events'' form a
decorative block rather than a calendar, so the count curve in $D_3$ is multiplied by a factor
reflecting whether any of them carries a date:
\begin{equation}
f_{\text{evt}}(c, t) = \underbrace{\begin{cases}
0.00 & c = 0\\ 0.55 & 1 \le c \le 2\\ 1.00 & 3 \le c \le 12\\
0.85 & 13 \le c < 20\\ 0.70 & c \ge 20
\end{cases}}_{\text{how many}} \times \underbrace{\begin{cases} 1.0 & t = 1\\ 0.6 & t = 0
\end{cases}}_{\text{are they dated?}}
\label{eq:evt}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Alt-text, concave.} Going from 0\% to 20\% of images labelled is a larger accessibility
gain than going from 80\% to 100\%, so the transform in $D_5$ is
\begin{equation}
f_{\text{alt}}(p) = \sqrt{p/100}\,, \qquad p \in [0,100].
\label{eq:alt}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Threats to the Validity of the Label}
\label{sec:threats}

The label is an arithmetic function of \nScored{} of the \nFeat{} features the model is given. This
is a serious limitation and the leading methodological risk in the study, and it bounds what the
modelling results can be said to demonstrate, so we state it here rather than leave a reader to
discover it.

\begin{keybox}{What the reported accuracy does and does not mean}
Because $y$ is computed from the features by Equation~\eqref{eq:score}, a flexible enough model can
in principle reconstruct it exactly. \textbf{The reported $R^2 = \tR{}$ is therefore not evidence
that website quality is predictable}; it is evidence that this particular function is recoverable
from the raw observables.

The question the report can legitimately answer is consequently narrower: given a scoring function
containing thresholds, saturations and two hard gates, which families of regression algorithm can
recover it from the raw attributes, and why do the others fail? That question is not circular, and
Section~\ref{sec:why} answers it with a mechanism rather than a leaderboard. Three properties of the
design keep it meaningful. The model never sees the sub-scores, the curve outputs or the gate flag,
only the raw attributes, so it has to recover the structure rather than read it off. \nUnscored{}
features do not enter the label at all, so it also has to work out what is irrelevant. And recovery
is demonstrably not automatic: $R^2$ across the eleven non-trivial algorithms spans \rTwoMin{} to
\rTwoMax{}, with the spread concentrated on an identifiable subpopulation.
\end{keybox}

\noindent Two consequences follow, and Section~\ref{sec:limitations} carries both. The ranking is a
separate artefact from the model, and its trustworthiness rests on the defensibility of the weights
rather than on $R^2$, which is why Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} tests the ranking directly. And the
gap between declared weight and realised importance in Section~\ref{sec:importance} is a statement
about which dimensions vary between universities in this dataset, not a discovery about quality. The
proper remedy is a label that is not a function of the features, either independent human ratings or
blind pairwise judgements fitted with a Bradley--Terry model; that was outside the scope of this
study and is the first item of future work.

\subsection{Train / Test Split and the Country Holdout}
\label{sec:split}

The dataset is split 80\% training (\nTrain{} universities) and 20\% testing (\nTest{}), stratified
on score band so both halves span the full quality range, with a fixed random seed, and with one
constraint imposed on top: \textbf{every one of the \nBD{} Bangladeshi universities is in the test
set, and none is used for training}.

There are two reasons. The first is that the case study has to mean one thing.
Section~\ref{sec:bangladesh} presents a table of all \nBD{} Bangladeshi universities with predicted
and actual scores; had some been in the training set, half the table would be a memory and half a
prediction, and the two halves would not be comparable row to row. The second is that this is a
stricter generalisation test than a random split. The \nBD{} sites are not a random draw: they share
a region, a hosting environment, a set of content-management templates and a set of design
conventions. Scoring them after seeing none of them tests whether the model learned the scoring
structure or the local habits of countries it had already met.

The rest of the test set is drawn band-stratified from the other 1,203 universities. We measured the
cost of the constraint rather than assuming it: the largest distortion to any grade band is
\maxDrift{} percentage points. The test set is used once, in Section~\ref{sec:final}; no algorithm,
hyper-parameter or threshold was chosen with it, and the build script asserts that no Bangladeshi
university reaches the training set so that a later edit cannot break the holdout silently.

\subsection{Algorithms, Validation Protocol and Metrics}

Twelve algorithms across six families were trained on identical data. Feature scaling is applied
inside a pipeline for the distance-, kernel- and gradient-based methods that need it, and
deliberately not for tree methods, which are invariant to monotone rescaling.

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\caption{The twelve algorithms, by family.}
\begin{tabular}{lp{9.2cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Family} & \textbf{Algorithms} \\
\midrule
Baseline   & Mean predictor \\
Linear     & Linear Regression, Ridge ($\alpha=1$), Lasso ($\alpha=0.1$) \\
Instance   & $k$-Nearest Neighbours ($k=5$) \\
Kernel     & Support Vector Regression (RBF, $C=100$) \\

\noindent Model selection and tuning use 5-fold cross-validation on the training set only, with

5-fold on the training set. Two baselines bound the results: the mean predictor sets the floor, since
any model failing to beat it has learned nothing, and linear regression sets the interpretable floor,
being what an additive model of the kind the prior literature uses can reach.

We report $R^2$ for variance explained, MAE in score points because it is directly interpretable,
RMSE because it penalises large errors, and Spearman $\rho$ for rank agreement, which matters most
given that the deliverable is a league table. For the case study we add Kendall $\tau$ and the number
of national positions predicted exactly.

\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Why the Algorithm Families Differ}
\label{sec:why}

All twelve algorithms saw identical features and identical training data, yet $R^2$ ranged from
\rTwoMin{} to \rTwoMax{}. Calling that a difference in model capacity explains nothing; the cause is
specific and can be demonstrated.

The label contains gates, the hard caps at 45 and 60 points in Equation~\eqref{eq:score}. A linear
model computes $\sum_j \beta_j x_j$ and has no way to say ``whatever else is true, stop here''. A
decision tree does, because a split is exactly that statement. If this is the mechanism, linear
models should fail specifically on gated universities and behave normally elsewhere. Splitting the
test error confirms it.

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\caption{Mean absolute error on the \nGatedTest{} gated versus \nUngatedTest{} ungated test
universities.}
\input{tables/tab_gate}
\end{table}

\begin{figure}[H]\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_gates.png}
\caption{Left: error split by whether a gate applied. Right: linear regression systematically
over-predicts the capped universities (red), because it sums the content the site does have and never
applies the cap.}
\end{figure}

\noindent Linear regression is wrong by \linGated{} points on the gated universities against 1.85
elsewhere, a factor of \linRatio{}. Every tree-based model is as good or better on the gated cases
than on the ungated ones, and LightGBM sits at \lgbGated{}. The gap between algorithm families is
therefore not a vague matter of capacity but the concrete inability of an additive model to represent
a threshold, visible on \nGatedTest{} specific universities. The direction of the error supports the
mechanism rather than merely correlating with it, since linear regression does not scatter around the
gated universities but over-predicts them.

\subsection{Feature Importance}
\label{sec:importance}

We compute importance by gain, meaning how much each split reduced the loss, rather than by split
count. The distinction matters: split-count importance flatters continuous attributes, which get
split many times, over binary ones, which are split once. Under split-count importance load time
looked like the single most important feature; under gain it accounts for well under 1\%.

\noindent A declared weight is not the same as realised influence. A dimension can differentiate

quality, but it echoes a known result in the website-evaluation literature where a scheme allocating
40\% of its score to performance was later found to drive under 3\% of its ranking variance.

The model also recovered the gating rule without being told about it. \texttt{a02\_primary\_nav}
accounts for \impNav{} of all gain, far beyond its arithmetic contribution of 3.75 points inside

Together with \texttt{a37\_programs\_listing} at \impProg{}, two of \nFeat{} features account for over
half of everything the model uses.

partitions of the feature set agreeing is evidence that the ablation measures something real rather
than an artefact of how the blocks were drawn.

\subsection{Sensitivity of the Ranking to the Weights}
\label{sec:sensitivity}

The weights are a documented judgement and a different analyst would choose differently. The honest
question is not whether they are correct, since there is no ground truth to be correct against, but

and the whole ranking recomputed, 300 draws at each of four levels. In the second, each dimension is
deleted outright, which is a far more violent change than any plausible disagreement.

\noindent The MAE on Bangladesh, \bdMAE{}, is about half the MAE on the rest of the test set,
\bdRestMAE{}, which is the opposite of what a country holdout usually produces. It is not leakage,
since the build script asserts there is none, and it is not a paradox. It follows from where these
\nBD{} universities sit: \bdBandN{} of them fall between \bdBandLo{} and \bdBandHi{} points, a dense
band the model has seen from a thousand other universities. The model's errors concentrate at the
extremes of the score range, and only three Bangladeshi sites are down there, the three that hit the
navigation gate, all of which the model places correctly near 45. We read this as showing that the
scoring structure learned elsewhere transfers to a country the model never trained on, and that
Bangladeshi university websites are structurally ordinary rather than a distinct population needing
their own model. It does not show that the model would transfer as well to a country structurally
unlike anything in the training set.

For a league table the ordering matters more than the score, and the model reproduces the national
ordering at Spearman $\rho = \bdSpear{}$. The remaining disagreements are local rather than
structural, occurring inside tightly packed groups where several universities sit within about a point
of each other, which is the same order as the model's own error, and no university crosses more than
one grade boundary. The top of the national table, the middle group and the gated tail are all placed
correctly; the exact ordering within a cluster of near-identical sites is not something a model with
roughly one point of error can be expected to resolve.

\subsection{Prestige and Website Quality}
\label{sec:prestige}

Table~\ref{tab:bd} contains a result worth stating plainly, because it is the clearest justification
for excluding the external ranking values in Section~\ref{sec:data} and the ranking badges in
Section~\ref{sec:exclusions}. The Bangladesh University of Engineering and Technology, by any
conventional measure the country's most prestigious institution, ranks
\buetRank{}\textsuperscript{th} of \nBD{} on website quality with a score of \buetScore{} and a grade
of \buetGrade{}, while a private university founded in 1992 leads the national table. Institutional
prestige and landing-page quality are close to unrelated in this dataset, which is why a
website-quality ranking is worth computing separately rather than assuming existing rankings already
answer the question.

\section{Risks and Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{table}[H]\centering\small
\caption{The principal risks to the validity of this study, and how each is handled.}
\begin{tabular}{p{3.3cm}p{3.9cm}p{7.3cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Risk} & \textbf{Impact} & \textbf{Disposition} \\
\midrule
Label leakage: the label is an arithmetic function of the features &
Reported accuracy is circular if read as quality prediction &
\textbf{Not eliminated.} The research question is narrowed accordingly and the limitation stated in
full in Section~\ref{sec:threats}. Sub-scores, curve outputs and the gate flag are withheld from the
model, the \nUnscored{} unscored features are supplied as distractors, and the ranking is validated
independently of the model in Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} \\[3pt]
Prestige leakage via displayed QS / Webometrics values &
The model ranks universities rather than websites &
Ranking value columns dropped in cleaning, being 95--99\% null and leaky; the badge presence flags are
retained as page features but excluded from the label, and ablation shows the whole block is worth
almost nothing \\[3pt]
Inconsistent extraction across six collectors &
Systematic between-region offsets mistaken for quality differences &
Collector and region are perfectly confounded and this cannot be undone; load time is used only in
region-standardised form, and the confound is reported rather than adjusted for \\[3pt]
JavaScript-heavy sites under-extracted &
Content attributes systematically undercounted &
About 2\% of rows affected; they fall to the bottom of the ranking and are indistinguishable from
genuinely unnavigable sites \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The label is a function of the features}, as set out in Section~\ref{sec:threats}. It
bounds every accuracy figure in Sections~\ref{sec:results} and~\ref{sec:final} to a claim about
recoverability rather than about quality prediction.
\item \textbf{The dataset cannot see visual design.} Every attribute is a presence flag, a count or a
measurement, so whether a page is cluttered, dated-looking or unpleasant to use is not among the
\nFeat{} columns and cannot be in the label. This is also why seven media and visual attributes are
excluded from it. Closing this gap needs screenshots and human raters rather than more modelling.
\item \textbf{About 2\% of rows are extraction failures.} A small number of universities returned
almost nothing to the crawler, which is characteristic of JavaScript-rendered pages the extractor
could not read rather than of genuinely empty websites. Since the navigation gate is triggered by
exactly this failure mode, an extraction failure and a genuinely unnavigable site look the same to the
label, so the bottom of the ranking should not be quoted without this caveat.
\item \textbf{The score reflects the landing page}, not the whole site and not the institution. An
excellent admissions portal that the front page does not link to is invisible to this method.
\item \textbf{The weights are a documented judgement, not a measured truth.} A stated rule fixed them
before scoring and they are published in full, and Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity} quantifies the
consequence: at $\pm 30\%$ on every weight the ranking holds at $\rho = \sensRho{}$.
\item \textbf{Excluding \nUnscored{} attributes is itself a judgement.} Each exclusion has a stated
reason (Table~\ref{tab:excl}), but reasons can be disagreed with. The most arguable is
\texttt{a28\_contests}, excluded as an engagement signal rather than an information need despite
correlating with the score at 0.29.
\item \textbf{The Bangladesh result is one country}, not a general claim about transfer. The \nBD{}
Bangladeshi sites resemble their South Asian neighbours, of which the training set contains many.
\item \textbf{Region and data collector are perfectly confounded}, so regional differences in a
measurement such as load time cannot be separated from differences in how it was measured.
\item \textbf{The crawl is a single snapshot.} Websites change continuously, the freshness attributes
in particular would differ on another day, and $D_3$ is the least stable dimension in the label over
time.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

We collected and audited a global corpus of \nUni{} landing pages described by \nFeat{} attributes,
classified every attribute by page region, measurement type and scoring role, and built a
seven-dimension label grounded in what a prospective student needs, derived by task decomposition and
stated as equations with every inner weight, response curve and gating rule published, along with a
reason for each of the \nUnscored{} attributes the label does not use. Twelve regression algorithms
were then trained to predict that label from the raw attributes. The tuned LightGBM model reaches
$R^2 = \tR{}$ with a mean absolute error of \tMAE{} points on a test set held out from every fitting
and tuning decision, ordering the universities at Spearman $\rho = \tSpear{}$.

The comparative study is the more instructive half. Performance across the twelve algorithms was not a
smooth gradient of capability but a split along one structural line: models that can represent a
threshold and models that cannot. Isolating the \nGatedTest{} gated universities made the mechanism
visible, with linear regression wrong by \linGated{} points on exactly those cases against 1.85
elsewhere while LightGBM sits at \lgbGated{}, and gain-based importance confirmed that the winning
model had independently identified the gating attribute and given it \impNav{} of all loss reduction.
The ablation study reached the same conclusion from the opposite direction: deleting the six
navigation features costs more test accuracy than deleting the six academic ones, despite academic
information carrying nearly twice the declared weight.

The country holdout is the strongest evidence that the framework generalises. All \nBD{} Bangladeshi
universities were excluded from training by construction, and on them the model achieves MAE
$= \bdMAE{}$ and reproduces the national ordering at $\rho = \bdSpear{}$ with a worst-case error of
\bdMaxErr{} points. The case study also yields an actionable diagnosis: Bangladeshi university
websites sit above the world average and are strong on academic content, and what separates them from
the global top 100 is navigation and admission support, worth \bdGapNavAdm{} of the
\bdGapTop{}-point gap.

Three methodological points generalise beyond this dataset. Missing values should be filled according
to what absence means rather than by a default statistic, since median-filling one attribute here
would have described 468 abandoned notice boards as updated yesterday. A declared weight is not a
realised influence: the three lowest-weighted dimensions account for a negligible share of what the
trained model uses, simply because almost every university scores alike on them. And a subjective
design choice is not a reason to leave it unmeasured, since the weights are a judgement but the
sensitivity of the ranking to that judgement is an experiment we could run, and did.

The limitation of Section~\ref{sec:threats} should be carried out of this report along with its
results. The label is an arithmetic function of the features, so the accuracy figures measure
recoverability rather than quality prediction. What the study establishes is narrower than the
headline number suggests and, because the mechanism behind the spread between algorithm families is
identified rather than merely reported, more durable.

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Replace the constructed label with an independent one.} Human ratings on a stratified
subset, or blind pairwise judgements fitted with a Bradley--Terry model, would give a target that is
not a function of the features and would turn the accuracy figures from recoverability into genuine
prediction. This is the highest-value extension and addresses Section~\ref{sec:threats} directly.
\item \textbf{Add visual features.} Screenshot-derived attributes and a small panel of human raters
would address limitation 2 and would let the seven media and visual attributes currently excluded from
the label be scored on quality rather than presence.
\item \textbf{Replace the extractor with a headless browser} to eliminate the JavaScript rendering
failures and with them the confusion between an unnavigable site and an unreadable one.
\item \textbf{Generalise the country holdout.} Extending the protocol of Section~\ref{sec:bangladesh}
to every country with at least 20 universities would turn a single case study into a
leave-one-country-out generalisation study.
\end{enumerate}
